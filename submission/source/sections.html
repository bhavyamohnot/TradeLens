How TradeLens was prompted into existence

Two rounds. A first version was built quickly with ChatGPT (Codex); it worked, but answered the wrong question. It was then audited against the brief and rebuilt and refined with Claude (Claude Code).

What the audit of the Round 1 app found

  • Wrong #1 habit. Findings appeared in a fixed order, not by ₹. Its single "next step" pointed at fees (₹2,100) while BANKNIFTY had cost ₹9,100.
  • Flagged a habit that made money. It called re-entries after a loss a problem, but those trades made +₹650.
  • Wrong answers in chat. "What time of day am I worst?" returned the strongest hour, which was itself −₹1,390.
  • Couldn't read order history. It needed closed trades with P&L already worked out; the brief starts from order history.
  • A dashboard, not a review. Metrics first, chat second, and no single "fix this one thing" outcome.
Lesson carried into Round 2: "build an agentic chatbot" described a technology, not the user's job. The rebuild started from the outcome instead: the 2–3 habits that cost the most rupees, each proven with trades and turned into one rule.

Round 2 · Claude (Claude Code): audit, then rebuild

Round 2 opened by pointing Claude at the brief, the JD, the example file and the live Round 1 app, and asking for an audit. The findings above became the spec. The rebuild was then driven step by step; the next page distils those steps into a prompt sequence that recreates TradeLens from scratch.

Artifact 02 · Prompts

Prompts to recreate TradeLens, step by step

The Round 2 build distilled into a sequence you can run in order with a coding agent. Each step names an outcome and a way to check it.

1
Audit before building
Here is the assignment PDF, the JD, an example trades file and a first prototype. Check the prototype against every requirement and against the example data: what should the top habit be, and what does the app actually say? List what it meets and misses. No code yet.
2
Frame the product
Problem 03: turn a month of order history into the 2–3 habits costing a trader money. Define a "habit" in measurable terms, what the output looks like (at most 3, ranked by ₹, each with the trades that prove it and one rule to try), and what's out of scope. Plain words, no motive-guessing.
3
Read real broker files
In TypeScript, read CSV/XLSX, find the header row even under title rows, and auto-map columns. Accept a broker tradebook (one row per buy/sell, Zerodha Console style) or a closed-trade sheet. Rebuild round trips per symbol: scale-ins, partial exits, shorts, flips through zero. Leave open positions out and say so. If there's no charges column, estimate NSE charges and label them as estimates.
4
Write the habit checks
Write pure-function detectors that return the trades involved and the ₹ fixing each would have saved: an instrument that keeps losing, trading on after 3 straight losses, re-entering ≤15 min after a loss, trades past your usual daily count, a losing time of day, options opened on expiry day, losers held ≥2× longer than winners, sizing up after a loss, charges ≥30% of the move.
5
Rank by rupees, and don't invent problems
Rank by marginal ₹: each habit only gets credit for trades a higher-ranked habit hasn't claimed. Show a habit only if it covers ≥3 trades, ≥5% of the month's losses, and is statistically worse than the trader's other trades. For everything checked and not shown, say why in one line: made money, too small, not significant, or overlapping.
6
Design a review, not a dashboard
Build a React page: upload and samples; a column check only when detection is unsure; then a verdict sentence, three habit cards (₹ cost, evidence, rule, trade list), one chart of the month with and without the habits, the "checked" list, and small stats last. Mobile first, INR formatting, everything in the browser.
7
Close the loop
Let the trader commit to one rule. Next month, compare that habit's ₹ cost with the month they committed.
8
Make a realistic month of data
Generate a deterministic September 2026 Zerodha-style tradebook: NIFTY weeklies with Tuesday expiries, BANKNIFTY monthlies, RELIANCE futures, equity intraday. Plant 3 habits of known cost plus decoys that must not be flagged, and write an answer key computed from the fills, not from the app.
9
Write the evals and a ship bar
Fixed cases (the brief's example, the sample, one per habit, a clean trader, a symbol-vs-time confound, messy formats), seeded sweeps (20 traders per habit, 30 clean traders), and copy checks on every review. Fail the run if the ship bar isn't met.
10
Fix honestly, then check on unseen data
Run the evals. For each failure, decide whether the engine or the test is wrong before changing anything, and never loosen a threshold just to pass. Then rerun on a held-out seed set you didn't tune on.
11
Ship
Make it a static Vite app on Vercel with no backend and no environment variables. Test both samples on the live URL at desktop and phone width.

Artifact 03 · Evals

How I decided it was good enough to ship

Every ₹ figure comes from deterministic code, so every claim can be tested. A single command, npm run eval, runs everything and fails if the ship bar isn't met.

Acceptance on the example data

  • Net −₹5,090 (gross −₹2,990, charges ₹2,100) ✓
  • One habit: BANKNIFTY kept losing, ₹9,100 ✓
  • Re-entries cleared: they made +₹650 ✓
  • Closing hour cleared once BANKNIFTY is set aside ✓
  • Weekend-dated trades flagged as a data warning ✓
September 2026 tradebook (388 fills)
  • 158 round trips, 1 open position set aside ✓
  • #1 Expiry-day options ₹30,131 · #2 Quick re-entry ₹15,932 · #3 Holding losers ₹11,916, matching the answer key ✓
  • No decoy flagged: profitable opening half hour, RELIANCE ✓

What the evals caught, and what changed

  1. Expiry-day losses were hidden under "NIFTY keeps losing" in 25% of traders. The engine now prefers the narrower habit when it explains most of the cost (75% → 100%).
  2. 10% false alarms on clean traders, all from checks that test many variants at once (5 time windows, every instrument). A stricter bar for those cut it to 0% (3% unseen).
  3. "Holding losers" overstated its cost, then was masked by other habits' fast losses. It is now measured only after higher habits claim their trades, so its evidence and ₹ agree.
  4. Twice the test was wrong, not the app: the example's longest losing streak is 21, not 20; and a planted habit sat exactly on the 5% materiality line. Both were fixed in the tests, with reasons recorded.

Limits: the data is synthetic; thresholds are judgement calls; only Zerodha-style tradebooks and generic closed-trade sheets are tested; charges are estimates when the file has none.

What to look for

  • The verdict names the month's net result, the habits' total cost and what the month would have been without them.
  • Each habit card shows the ₹ cost, the evidence, a strip of the month's trades with the habit's trades highlighted, one rule, and "Commit to this rule".
  • "Checked, not costing you money" shows the patterns that were tested and cleared, so the tool visibly doesn't invent problems.
  • Data notes cover estimated charges and positions left open. The review downloads as Markdown or prints to PDF.
